# Word Order — Cross-Linguistic Comparison

## Basic Word Order

| Language | Default | Flexibility | Key Rule |
|----------|---------|------------|----------|
| 🇨🇿 Czech | SVO | Very free | Topic-first, pragmatic ordering |
| 🇵🇱 Polish | SVO | Very free | Same as Czech — new info at end |
| 🇩🇪 German | V2 | Structured | Verb ALWAYS in 2nd position (main clause) |
| 🇷🇺 Russian | SVO | Very free | Same as CZ/PL — pragmatic |
| 🇬🇧 English | SVO | Very rigid | Almost no flexibility |

## The Big Difference: German V2 Rule

German is the ONLY one of your five languages with a strict verb-position rule:

### Main clause: Verb in 2nd position (V2)
```
✅ Ich gehe morgen ins Kino.        (I go tomorrow to-the cinema)
✅ Morgen gehe ich ins Kino.         (Tomorrow go I to-the cinema)
✅ Ins Kino gehe ich morgen.         (To-the cinema go I tomorrow)
```
The verb "gehe" is ALWAYS in position 2, no matter what comes first!

### Subordinate clause: Verb at the END
```
✅ ..., weil ich morgen ins Kino gehe.  (because I tomorrow to-the cinema go)
✅ ..., dass er nach Hause kommt.       (that he to home comes)
```

### Compare with Czech/Polish (no such rule):
```
CZ: Já jdu zítra do kina. / Zítra jdu do kina. / Do kina jdu zítra.
PL: Ja idę jutro do kina. / Jutro idę do kina. / Do kina idę jutro.
```
All equally natural — verb can be anywhere!

## Slavic Word Order: Information Structure

Czech, Polish, and Russian all use the same pragmatic principle:

**Old/known information → first, New/important information → last**

| What you say | Czech | Polish |
|-------------|-------|--------|
| "Peter bought a car" (news: the car) | Petr koupil **auto**. | Piotr kupił **samochód**. |
| "A car was bought by Peter" (news: Peter) | Auto koupil **Petr**. | Samochód kupił **Piotr**. |

This is identical in all three Slavic languages and VERY different from English (which uses passive voice for this) or German (which uses V2 + different emphasis).

## Negative Sentences

| Language | Pattern | Example |
|----------|---------|---------|
| CZ | ne + verb | **Ne**rozumím. |
| PL | nie + verb | **Nie** rozumiem. |
| DE | nicht (or kein) | Ich verstehe **nicht**. |
| RU | не + verb | Я **не** понимаю. |
| EN | do + not + verb | I **don't** understand. |

> **CZ+PL+RU:** Negation particle directly before verb — identical logic!
> **DE:** "nicht" goes to the END of clause (usually)
> **EN:** Needs auxiliary "do" — most complex negation

## Question Formation

| Language | Yes/No question | Method |
|----------|----------------|--------|
| CZ | Mluvíš česky? / Mluvíš česky? | Intonation (or optional "Jestli") |
| PL | Czy mówisz po polsku? / Mówisz po polsku? | "Czy" particle (optional) + intonation |
| DE | Sprichst du Deutsch? | Verb moves to 1st position! |
| RU | Ты говоришь по-русски? | Intonation only |
| EN | Do you speak English? | Auxiliary "do" + inversion |

> **CZ+PL+RU:** Just use intonation! PL has optional "czy" particle.
> **DE:** Verb-first for questions (V1 instead of V2)
> **EN:** Needs "do" auxiliary

## Adjective Position

| Language | Position | Example |
|----------|----------|---------|
| CZ | Before noun | **Velký** dům |
| PL | Before noun | **Duży** dom |
| DE | Before noun | **Großes** Haus |
| RU | Before noun | **Большой** дом |
| EN | Before noun | **Big** house |

> ALL five languages put adjectives before the noun! (Unlike French, Spanish, Italian)
> This is one less thing to worry about.

## Clitic Position (Advanced)
Czech, Polish, and partly Russian have special rules for small unstressed words (clitics: se, si, by, mi, ti, etc.):

CZ: Clitics go in **2nd position** (Wackernagel position):
- ✅ Petr **se** díval na film. (Petr REFL watched at film)
- ✅ Na film **se** Petr díval.

PL: Similar but less strict than CZ.

This doesn't exist in German or English.
